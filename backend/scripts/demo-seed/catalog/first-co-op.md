# Getting ready to apply

A co-op search goes more smoothly when your materials are ready before you start applying. Three things matter most: a good relationship with your co-op advisor, a resume aimed at the roles you want, and a place where employers can see your work.

## Meet your co-op advisor early

Your co-op advisor knows how the process works in your program and has helped other students through it. Meet them before you start applying, and come prepared:

- Bring a draft of your resume.
- Bring a short list of roles or industries you're curious about, even if you're not sure yet.
- Ask how the process and timeline work for your program.
- Ask what has helped past students with goals like yours.

%important Keep your co-op advisor updated as things happen, from interviews to offers to problems. They can only help with what they know about.

## Tailor your resume

You probably have a general resume already. For co-op, the key step is tailoring it:

- Keep a master resume with every project, job and activity you've done.
- For each kind of role, make a version that leads with your most relevant work. A data analyst version might open with your SQL project, and a software version with the app your team built.
- Use the posting's words where they're true. If it asks for REST API experience and you built one, say "REST API" rather than something vaguer like "backend work".

%warning Only list skills you can answer questions about. Anything on your resume is fair game in an interview.

## Show your work

Before your first co-op, your projects are your experience. Make them easy to find:

- Pin your best repositories to your GitHub profile.
- Give each one a README that says what it does, how to run it, and which parts you built, especially for group projects.
- Clean up before you share: remove passwords and API keys, and make sure the code runs.
- If your work is visual, like a club website, a simple portfolio page with screenshots and links works well.

# Applying and interviewing

Once you start applying, the work is keeping track, tailoring each application, and getting ready to talk about yourself.

## Track every application

A simple spreadsheet keeps you from losing track of where each application stands:

| Role | Organization | Status | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Software engineering co-op | Health tech startup | Interview scheduled | Prepare three STAR stories |
| Data analyst co-op | Hospital research lab | Applied | Follow up if no reply by Friday |
| IT support co-op | University department | Offer received | Talk to your co-op advisor before the deadline |

Add columns for the date you applied, deadlines and contacts if they help. Update the sheet every time something changes.

## Tailor each application

Read each posting closely before you apply. Pick the two or three requirements that matter most, and make sure your resume shows evidence of each one near the top. If you write a cover letter, connect one specific project to what the team does. Tailoring takes time, so spend it on roles you actually want.

## Answer behavioral questions with STAR

Interviews often include behavioral questions, like "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate." The interviewer wants one specific story, not a general claim like "I'm a team player."

%definition The STAR method structures a story in four parts: Situation, Task, Action and Result.

Here's a STAR answer about a group project:

1. **Situation:** In my CS 3200 group project, one teammate stopped replying two weeks before the deadline.
2. **Task:** Our reporting page depended on their queries, so we needed a plan to finish on time.
3. **Action:** I messaged them directly and learned they had three exams that week. We moved two of their queries to me, and I set up a 15-minute check-in twice a week.
4. **Result:** We submitted on time, earned an A, and kept the check-ins for the rest of the semester.

A few habits make STAR answers stronger:

- Spend most of your answer on the Action, and say "I" for the things you did.
- Prepare four or five stories from projects, jobs, clubs and classes. You can adapt each one to several questions.
- Practice out loud, and aim for about two minutes per answer.
- Have two or three questions ready to ask the interviewer at the end.

# Choosing an offer and starting well

An offer is good news, and it's also a decision. Take the time to choose well, then set yourself up for a strong first few weeks.

## Comparing offers

Pay matters, but it isn't the only thing to compare:

- **The work.** What will you build or analyze, and what will you learn?
- **The people.** Who will your manager be, and will you have a mentor or other co-ops nearby?
- **Logistics.** Location, commute, hours, and whether the role is in person, hybrid or remote.
- **Your goals.** How does the role set you up for your next co-op or your first job?

## Questions to ask before you accept

It's normal to ask an employer questions before you decide. Good ones include:

- "What would a typical week look like for me?"
- "What did your last co-op work on?"
- "Who would I work with day to day, and how does the team give feedback?"
- "Which tools and languages does the team use?"

%important Talk to your co-op advisor before you accept or turn down an offer. They can explain how offers and deadlines work in your program.

Treat accepting an offer as a firm commitment. Backing out later lets down a team that planned around you, so accept only when you're ready to commit.

## Your first weeks

The first few weeks set the tone for the rest of your co-op:

- Try a problem on your own first, then ask for help and explain what you've tried.
- Write things down: setup steps, names, acronyms and answers, so you don't have to ask twice.
- Ask your manager what a successful co-op looks like to them, and set up a regular check-in.
- Ask for feedback a few weeks in, not just at the end.
- Keep a weekly log of what you worked on and learned. It makes your next resume and interviews much easier to prepare.
