# Seeing where your time goes

Most plans fall apart because they're built on a guess about how much free time you have. Before you plan, find out where your hours actually go.

## Track one ordinary week

Pick a normal week, not exam week or a holiday. A few times a day, write down what you did since your last check, in rough 30-minute chunks. A notes app, a spreadsheet or your calendar all work.

Be honest. Twenty minutes of scrolling between classes counts, and so does the walk to the library. You're collecting information, not grading yourself.

## Start with fixed commitments

%definition A fixed commitment is anything that happens at a set time you can't easily move, like a lecture, a lab or a work shift.

Add your fixed commitments to your calendar as repeating events: classes, labs, work shifts, club meetings, commuting, meals and sleep. Then add up the hours. Here's one student's week:

| Commitment | Hours per week |
|---|---|
| Sleep | 56 |
| Classes and labs | 16 |
| Campus job | 10 |
| Meals | 10 |
| Commuting | 5 |
| Club meetings | 3 |
| Total | 100 |

A week has 168 hours, so this student has 68 left for homework, studying, errands, exercise, friends and rest. That's the real budget you're planning with.

## Look for patterns

Once you have a week of notes, look for three things:

- **Your best hours.** When do you focus most easily? Save hard work, like problem sets, for those hours.
- **Leaks.** Short gaps, phone checks and tasks that took twice as long as expected add up fast.
- **Hidden time.** A 50-minute gap between classes is enough to finish a reading or review your notes.

# Planning your week

A plan turns "I should work on the project" into a specific task at a specific time. Two habits do most of the work: a weekly review and time blocking.

## The weekly review

Pick a fixed time each week, like Sunday evening, and spend half an hour looking ahead:

1. Collect every deadline, exam and meeting for the next two weeks from your syllabi and course sites.
2. Add them to your calendar.
3. Choose the few tasks that matter most this week.
4. Block time for each one.
5. Look back at last week. What didn't get done, and why?

## Time blocking

Time blocking means putting tasks on your calendar like appointments. Be specific: "CS 3200 homework 4, questions 1 to 3" is a block you can start right away, and "study" isn't.

- Put your hardest work in your best-focus hours.
- Leave empty buffer time. People routinely underestimate how long their own tasks will take, a pattern psychologists call the planning fallacy.
- When you miss a block, move it to a new time instead of deleting it.

## Breaking big assignments into dated steps

A project due in three weeks feels like it can wait, until suddenly it can't. Break it into steps and give each step its own date. Here's a plan for a group database project due on Friday of week 3:

| Step | Done by |
|---|---|
| Read the spec and list questions for the TA | Monday, week 1 |
| Agree on the schema with your team | Thursday, week 1 |
| Write and test the SQL queries | Wednesday, week 2 |
| Draft the report | Monday, week 3 |
| Review together and submit | Thursday, week 3 |

The last step lands a day before the deadline, which leaves room for something to go wrong.

%important If a step is too big to finish in one or two sittings, break it down again.

# Protecting your focus

A plan only works if the time you block actually goes to the task. Three habits protect it: doing one thing at a time, keeping your phone away, and working in focused sessions.

## One task at a time

Each time you switch tasks, it takes a while to get back into the first one. Research on task switching consistently finds that jumping back and forth makes work slower and more error-prone than finishing one thing at a time.

- Close the tabs and apps you don't need for the current task.
- Keep a "later" list. When something unrelated pops into your head, write it down and get back to work.

## Put your phone out of reach

If your phone is on the desk, you'll check it. Put it in your bag or another room, and turn on Do Not Disturb or a focus mode while you work. If your laptop is the problem, log out of distracting sites or use a site blocker during work blocks.

## Work in focused sessions

The Pomodoro Technique gives your work a simple rhythm:

1. Pick one task.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task.
3. When the timer rings, take a short break of about 5 minutes.
4. After four sessions, take a longer break.

The exact lengths matter less than the rhythm of focused work and real breaks. If 50 minutes of work and a 10-minute break suit you better, use that.

%caution A break that turns into an hour on your phone isn't a break. Stand up, stretch, refill your water or take a short walk instead.

## Saying no

Every new commitment takes time from something already on your calendar. Before you agree to an extra club role or a side project, check your week. Saying no can be short and polite:

- "I can't take that on this semester, but thanks for thinking of me."
- "I can help with the design, but I don't have time to lead it."
- "Let me check my week and get back to you tomorrow."
